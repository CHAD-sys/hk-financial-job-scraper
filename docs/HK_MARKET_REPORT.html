Labour-market evidence note · Hong Kong financial services

What 13,518 job listings can — and cannot — tell us about the Hong Kong finance market

A structural read of the live Hong Kong financial-services vacancy pool, built from eight scraped sources over eight weeks. Roughly half the interesting questions turn out to be unanswerable with this data. This note is explicit about which half.

Compiled 28 July 2026 Corpus 13,518 listings · 4,996 live Observation 31 May – 26 July 2026 Basis jobs.db + 10 dated snapshots

01 / Instrumentation

The measuring instrument changed more than the market did

This dataset was not collected by a stable instrument pointed at a moving market. It was collected by an instrument that was itself under construction throughout the observation window. Eight scrapers came online across eight weeks; the pay estimator was rewritten once; three enrichment fields did not exist for the first month.

That ordering matters because almost every naive question — "is hiring up?", "are salaries rising?", "which employers are growing?" — produces an answer dominated by when a scraper was switched on rather than by anything happening in Hong Kong. Disqualified before analysis

The daily company-count panel — the obvious basis for a hiring-trend chart — cannot support one. Across 55 calendar days it recorded only 34 runs. The company roster grew from 28 to 146 mid-series. 23 of the 34 runs covered fewer than half the roster, one covered a single company. And 21.3% of all 2,315 company-days are literal zeros, which conflate "this employer posted nothing" with "the scraper failed" — the two are indistinguishable in storage.

Any growth rate computed on this panel would mostly be reporting roster expansion. No such rate appears in this note.

Stripped of every longitudinal claim, the cross-section is solid. These are counts of live listings on a single date, and they do not depend on a stable observation window.

The dedup figure is the one worth internalising. Of 4,996 live listings only 3,612 are distinct roles: 27.7% of the pool is the same job advertised on more than one board. A naive union of these sources counts 1.38 listings for every real vacancy, so any headcount taken this way overstates demand by nearly 40%.

"Unique share" is the proportion of a source's live listings that are not duplicated elsewhere in the corpus. It separates sources that add roles from sources that mostly re-advertise them.

A statistic that looks like a market fact but isn't

17.9% of live listings disclose a salary. That number is entirely an artefact of source mix: eFinancialCareers discloses on 100% of its 849 listings, recruiter posts on 22%, and every other source on exactly 0%.

Hong Kong pay disclosure is not 18% — it is a property of which board you read. Reweighting the source mix would move the "disclosure rate" anywhere between 0% and 100% without a single employer changing behaviour.

03 / Pay structure

The seniority ladder is the market's dominant axis

Because only eFinancialCareers discloses pay, a market-wide salary picture has to come from model estimates. Those estimates are calibrated against published Hong Kong salary guides and clamped to them. Everything in this section is restricted to the 4,724 listings priced under one estimator version, so the comparison is internally consistent even though the level carries model error.

The overall median sits at HK$42,500/month, with an interquartile range of HK$30,000–60,500 and a 90th percentile of HK$76,000. The distribution is strongly right-skewed, which is why every figure in this note is a median rather than a mean.

04 / Skills

Most of the apparent skill premium is just seniority wearing a different hat

Comparing pay for listings that require a skill against those that don't is the standard move, and it is badly misleading. Senior roles list more skills and pay more, so any skill that skews senior inherits a premium it did not earn.

Correcting for that is straightforward: compute the median within each seniority band, then reweight to the overall seniority mix. The correction is not a rounding adjustment — across the 20 skills large enough to test, it moves the median premium from +2.6% to −2.2%. The typical "valuable" skill turns out to be worth nothing once you compare like with like.

One preliminary fix was needed first. Skill labels arrive case-fragmented — "Team Leadership", "Team leadership" and "team leadership" were stored as three separate skills with three different premia (+41%, +47%, +53%). Normalising collapsed 14,270 raw label strings into 10,914 distinct skills and roughly tripled the sample behind the common ones.

The three findings that survive adjustment

Private Banking is a genuine function premium. +41% raw, +41% adjusted — it barely moves, because the skill is split evenly across seniority bands. Someone is paying for the book of clients, not the years served.

Financial Modeling and Quantitative Analysis pay early. Both hold their premium (+24%, +27%) while being held disproportionately by junior staff — only 24% and 31% senior-held respectively. These are the rare skills that pay before you are promoted.

Regulatory Compliance is pure table stakes. It is the second most-demanded skill in the corpus, appearing in 744 listings, and it commands a premium of +0.1%. In Hong Kong finance, compliance is the price of entry, not a differentiator.

The mirror image is Team Leadership: +47% raw collapsing to +14% adjusted, with 73% of its listings senior. Roughly two-thirds of what looked like a leadership premium was simply the observation that senior people manage teams.

These are descriptive associations, not causal returns. Skills co-occur with function and employer as well as seniority; the adjustment removes one confounder, not all of them. Read them as "what listings requiring this skill pay", never as "what learning this skill would earn you".

05 / Model risk

When the estimator was rewritten, the market "lost" 16% of its pay overnight

On 21 July the pay estimator was re-run against a merged three-source salary reference. Dated database snapshots taken either side of that run allow the same 7,925 listings to be compared under both regimes — the listings did not change, only the model did.

55.1%Estimates changed4,365 of 7,925
−16.0%Median shift62,500 → 52,500
21.6%Median moveabsolute, changed only
3.3:1Down vs up3,345 vs 1,020

A prompt revision moved the median estimated salary by HK$10,000 a month — larger than most of the function gaps in section 03, and larger than any plausible eight-week market move. The revision was deliberate and corrective: the earlier estimator was running hot against published Hong Kong benchmarks, and the 3.3-to-1 ratio of downward to upward revisions is what a recalibration looks like.

Consequence for any pay time series

A chart of "average advertised salary over time" drawn across 21 July would show a 16% collapse. It would be entirely an artefact of the estimator. This is the single largest movement anywhere in the dataset, and the market did not produce it.

It also bounds the confidence available on levels: two defensible calibrations of the same listings disagree by 16% at the median and by 21.6% for the typical revised role. Treat all pay figures in section 03 as ordinally reliable — the rankings are stable — and as approximate in level.

06 / A negative result

Listing survival cannot be measured yet, and here is the proof

How long does a Hong Kong finance role stay open? The dated snapshots appear to answer this: track each listing until it disappears. Done naively, that yields a median open-duration of two days, which would be a remarkable finding.

It is an artefact. The test that breaks it is resurrection — checking whether listings that disappear ever come back.

Resurrection test

Nearly one listing in five disappears from a snapshot and reappears in a later one. Since a genuinely closed vacancy does not reopen, "absent" cannot be read as "closed" — it frequently means the scraper missed it.

The day-to-day churn confirms it. The share of live listings vanishing between consecutive runs swings from 3.2% to 19.4%, a six-fold range across adjacent days. Real vacancy closure does not behave that way; scrape completeness does.

Closure rates, time-to-fill and hiring velocity are therefore all withheld. They become measurable once the pipeline distinguishes "scrape returned nothing" from "employer posted nothing" — a change to how failures are recorded, not more data.

07 / Listing age

Half the market turns over inside a fortnight — and a tenth never turns over at all

One longitudinal question does survive, because it does not depend on our observation schedule at all. Each listing carries the date the employer posted it. The age distribution of the live pool is therefore measurable from a single snapshot.

The median live listing is 16 days old, with a quartile at five days and three-quarters of the pool under a month. That is a market that moves briskly at the centre.

The tail is the interesting part. 10.6% of live listings are more than 90 days old and 1.9% are more than a year old, the oldest dating from 2019. These are not necessarily open jobs. A listing this stale is usually one of three things: an evergreen pipeline advert that is never intended to close, a role filled long ago whose posting was never taken down, or a board that does not expire listings. All three inflate any headline count of "open roles" in Hong Kong finance — including the 4,996 at the top of this note.

Basis

1,815 recruiter posts from 45 tracked recruiters, 22–25 July.

Caveat
Four days of data. Composition only; no rates.

08 / The hidden market

Five in six recruiter-advertised roles never name the employer

Alongside the job boards, the pipeline reads public hiring posts by Hong Kong finance recruiters. This channel is small and very new — four days of collection — so only its composition is reported, never a rate or a trend.

1,815Posts read45 recruiters
579Yielded a role31.9% of posts
83.1%Employer withheld
100%Not on any board

Two things stand out. First, this channel is fully disjoint from the job boards — not one of its 197 live roles appears in the other seven sources. Whatever it represents, it is genuinely additional supply rather than a re-advertisement.

Second, 83.1% of these roles are advertised without naming the employer — "Confidential via —" and a recruiter's name. That is the defining property of the channel, and it is the mechanical reason this segment is invisible in conventional job-market statistics: employer-keyed analysis simply cannot see it. It also means the channel cannot be joined to the main corpus by employer, so cross-channel pay or demand comparisons are not available.

Two-thirds of recruiter posts contain no extractable role at all — they are commentary, congratulations and market chatter. A 31.9% yield is the realistic signal density of this source, and it should not be mistaken for a hiring indicator.

Grade

Reported with a health warning; employer labels are known to be contaminated.

09 / Employers

Concentration is real, but the employer labels are not clean

The live pool is moderately concentrated: the top five employers hold 33.7% of listings and the top ten hold 47.8%, across 162 employers. The Herfindahl index of 370 indicates a competitive market with a few dominant recruiters of scale — consistent with Hong Kong's retail-banking and insurance giants running continuous large-volume hiring.

Why this table carries a warning

162 employer keys carry 239 distinct name strings. 58 of them (35.8%) hold more than one name, and the worst holds seven. Some of that is benign — "PIMCO", "Pimco" and "PIMCO Hong Kong" are one firm.

Some of it is not. The zurich-hk key contains listings labelled "UBS AG"; bochk contains "OCBC Bank (Hong Kong) Limited". These are mis-attributed listings — an aggregator advertiser field being read as the employer. Counts for individual firms therefore carry an unquantified error in both directions, and the ranking above should be read as indicative of scale only, not as an audited league table.

10 / Limitations

What this data cannot tell us

Stated plainly, so that none of the figures above are over-read.

  • It cannot tell us whether hiring is rising or falling. The company roster grew from 28 to 146 during the window and 21.3% of company-days are unresolved zeros. No growth rate is recoverable, and the eight-week span would be too short for a seasonal read even if the panel were clean.
  • It cannot tell us how long roles stay open. The 18.7% resurrection rate means disappearance does not imply closure. Time-to-fill, closure rate and hiring velocity are all unavailable until scrape failures are recorded distinctly from genuine zeros.
  • It cannot tell us what anyone is actually paid. 82% of listings disclose no salary; the rest are model estimates calibrated to published guides. The 21 July recalibration moved the median 16%, which bounds how much confidence the levels deserve. Advertised pay is also not realised pay — it excludes bonus, which in Hong Kong front-office roles is a large share of total compensation.
  • It cannot tell us what a skill is worth to a person. The adjusted premia control for seniority only. Function, employer and unobserved candidate quality all remain confounded, and listings describe employer requirements rather than returns to training.
  • It cannot be read as the Hong Kong finance market. It is the subset that is publicly advertised on eight specific channels and matched to a configured employer list. Roles filled by internal transfer, executive search or direct referral never enter — and section 08 suggests that hidden segment is substantial.
  • Employer-level counts carry unquantified attribution error. 35.8% of employer keys hold multiple name strings and some contain a different institution's listings outright.
  • Two of the eight sources are collected in breach of site terms. The JobsDB and Indeed adapters are prototype-grade and would need a licensed feed or explicit permission before any of this is published externally.

What would unlock the withheld analysis

In priority order: record adapter failure distinctly from a genuine zero, so the panel stops conflating them; freeze the employer roster and the estimator version for a continuous 60-day run; deduplicate employer names against a canonical register; and version every derived field so that a series can be restricted to one regime automatically. None of these require more data — only that the instrument stop changing while it is being read.
