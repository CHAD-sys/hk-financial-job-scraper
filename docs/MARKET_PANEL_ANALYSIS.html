FINDINGS

The short version

This dataset is strong where people rarely look and weak exactly where the tempting headline lives. The cross-section — what roles exist right now, what they pay, what they ask for — is solid and worth building on. The time series — who is hiring more this week than last — is not yet trustworthy, and publishing a "who's hiring" leaderboard from it today would be reporting scraper behaviour as market behaviour.

01 — INTEGRITY

Start with what the panel cannot support

The obvious first question — which banks are ramping up? — is the one question this panel currently answers worst. Three separate defects compound, and each one alone would be enough to invalidate a trend claim.

Defect one: a quarter of all observations are zero

Across the 146-company stable window, 323 of 1,314 company-days record exactly zero jobs. These are not employers with an empty careers page — they are adapter failures. AXA Hong Kong reads 44, 48, 0, 53, 0, 52, 47, 61, 62 on consecutive scrapes. Zurich reads 18, 20, 0, 20, 0, 20. A zero followed by a full recovery is a fetch that failed, recorded as a fact.

Defect two: a pagination fingerprint

Counts cluster on exactly 10 and exactly 20 far more than chance allows — 56 and 29 observations respectively, with 22 companies landing on those two values repeatedly. Those are page sizes, not job counts. Goldman Sachs reads 59, 49, 10, 10, 18, 10, 36, 17, 10; Standard Chartered reads 45, 67, 10, 20, 72, 19, 45, 72, 10. When a paginated fetch stops after page one, the panel records a collapse in hiring.

Defect three: the delta columns re-seed

jobs_added is meant to hold the day-over-day increase. In 9.7% of observations it instead equals the full job count, re-declaring every open role as new. HKEX moved from 119 to 124 roles on 23 July and logged jobs_added = 124. Summing that column across the window produces "HKEX added 244 roles" — an artefact of the same five-role change counted twice over.

Consequence

The hiring-velocity and net-mover analyses were computed, inspected, and discarded. They are not in this report. Until the zero-guard and pagination fixes land, any company-level trend read from job_history is measuring the scraper.

A fourth, milder caveat: the roster grew

The panel opens on 1 June tracking 28 companies and closes tracking 146. Total job counts therefore rise for reasons that have nothing to do with the market. Every time-based figure in this report is confined to the stable-roster window of 16–25 July, where the roster is constant at 146 — nine scrape dates, not the full seven weeks.

02 — TIME ON MARKET

How long a posting actually stays open

Because fetched_at updates on every run, the last time a posting was seen alive is recoverable, and the gap from posted_at gives its observed life. That invites an obvious mistake, which this section makes and then corrects: the sources have not been scraped for the same length of time.

A correction, shown rather than hidden

A first pass computed median observed life per source and reported 28 days for employer ATS feeds against 2 days for eFinancialCareers. That comparison is invalid. Workday and Eightfold have been scraped since 31 May — a 55-day window. eFinancialCareers has been scraped only since 14 July, an 11-day window. A posting cannot be observed to live 28 days inside 11 days of observation, so the only eFC postings that can appear "closed" are the short-lived ones. The rest are still open and silently excluded.

The giveaway was sitting in the data: eFC currently has 86 active listings more than 30 days old, which no 2-day median can survive.

The fix is to measure every source the same way: take only postings with at least seven full days of follow-up, and ask what share had disappeared by day seven. That question is well defined for any source, whatever its window.

But "gone from the board" is not "role filled"

Before reading fast churn as fast hiring, one more test. For every eFC listing that closed since 1 July, does the same employer still have an identical job title live under a different listing ID? For 29.2% of them it does — 81 of 277. HKEX and DBS rotate listing IDs constantly, cycling the same Treasures Relationship Manager and Post-Trade Systems roles through new IDs. Because that test requires an exact title match and only counts roles still live today, 29.2% is a floor, not an estimate.

The corrected finding

Listing churn measures a board's hygiene, not whether a job was filled. Each source's disappearance rate is mostly a property of its own policy: JobsDB delists on a 30-day timer, eFinancialCareers rotates IDs, Indeed leaves dead listings up for years. Only employer-ATS feeds track the actual role, and they say finance roles stay open for weeks — under 6% close within a week.

03 — RHYTHM

The weekly cycle, and the day that isn't real

Hong Kong finance posts on weekdays. Per weekday the panel sees 1,785 postings against 509 per weekend day — a 3.5× ratio that holds across every source independently, including the employer ATS feeds where the timestamp is written by the employer's own system. Eightfold recorded zero weekend postings in the entire window.

Testing the Thursday peak

A chi-square test against a uniform weekday distribution returns χ² = 21.51 on 4 df, comfortably past the 9.49 critical value. Statistically significant — and with 9,941 observations, almost any wobble would be. So the real test is whether the effect replicates across independent sources. It does not.

04 — NULL RESULT

The "apply in the first hour" rule fails its test

The most repeated piece of job-hunting advice is that competition compounds fast, so the earliest applicants win. The panel carries LinkedIn applicant counts for 665 live postings with usable dates, which is enough to test it directly.

What this does and doesn't mean

It does not mean applying early is harmful. It means this dataset provides no evidence that a same-day application beats a considered one submitted three days later — and since under 6% of employer-ATS roles close within a week, the time cost of a careful application is small.

One survivorship caveat, stated plainly: we only observe applicant counts on postings still open. If heavily-applied roles close faster, the crowded ones drop out of view and the true age-competition slope is partly hidden. The honest conclusion is that the panel cannot support the urgency claim, not that urgency has been disproven.

05 — COVERAGE

The board is smaller, and more fragmented, than it looks

Of 4,986 active rows, 2,304 are flagged as cross-posted and 1,375 of those are suppressed as non-primary duplicates. The board therefore represents roughly 3,611 distinct roles — a 28% gap between the row count and the opportunity count.

Worth flagging for the engineering side: exact dedup_hash matching finds only 146 jobs on two sources. The other ~2,150 cross-post detections come entirely from the fuzzy title matcher. That matcher is carrying the whole feature, and it has never been precision-audited against a labelled sample. If it over-merges, real distinct roles are being hidden from users.

07 — SECRET MARKET

The recruiter channel is working, and it is nearly free

The paid LinkedIn recruiter-post channel has ingested 578 posts and promoted 196 into live roles, 100% of which appear on no other source. Cost to date is US$4.21 against the US$30 monthly cap — 14% of budget for roughly 4% of the active board, in the segment least visible to candidates using public boards.

08 — APPLICATION

So when is the best time to apply?

"When" turns out to be the wrong axis almost entirely. The panel finds no measurable timing effect on competition, and the apparent urgency differences between sources dissolve into listing-hygiene policies once measured properly. What is left is a short, unglamorous list.

Employer
ATS

You have weeks. Use them on quality. Under 6% of Workday and Eightfold postings close within a week, and under 8% within two. HSBC, AIA, Bank of China and the other ATS employers are not going to fill the role before you finish writing. This is the only source class whose dates track the actual job.

Aggregator
boards

Never read urgency into a disappearance. A JobsDB listing dies on a 30-day timer; an eFinancialCareers listing rotates its ID with the role still open at least 29% of the time; Indeed leaves dead listings up for years. A vanished listing is weak evidence that a role is gone.

eFC
specifically

If a listing vanishes, search the employer again. Roughly a third of eFC closures reappear under a new ID within days — the same HKEX and DBS roles cycling. Losing the link is not losing the opportunity.

Any
source

Check on weekdays; skip the weekend refresh. New supply runs 3.5× higher per weekday, and Monday through Friday are statistically indistinguishable from each other. There is no magic day, and no evidence that applying within the hour helps.

60-day
tail

Treat listings over two months old with suspicion. 15.6% of the live board is 60-plus days old. On employer-ATS evidence most roles resolve well inside that, so much of the tail is filled roles nobody retracted.

The one place urgency and value genuinely coincide is the recruiter channel: those 196 roles are, by construction, invisible to everyone applying through public boards.

09 — NEXT

What would make the next report stronger

Ranked by how much analytical capability each unlocks per unit of work. The first two convert an unusable time series into a usable one.

  1. Never write a zero on a failed fetch. Have the adapter raise rather than return an empty list, and record the company-day as missing instead of zero. This alone repairs 24.6% of observations and is the single highest-value change on this list.
  2. Assert full pagination before persisting. If a fetch returns exactly one page for a company that historically returns several, treat it as a partial failure. The 10-and-20 spike disappears and the volatile-company share should fall well below 32.9%.
  3. Fix the jobs_added re-seed. Compute the delta against the previous observation for that company; never fall back to the full count when a prior row exists. Removes the 9.7% corruption and makes net-mover analysis possible.
  4. Add a first_seen_at column, and record each adapter's start date. Posting lifespan is currently reconstructed from posted_at, which relies on source-supplied dates of wildly varying quality — and nothing in the schema records how long each source has been observed. That omission is exactly what produced the retracted 2-day figure in section 02. A first-seen timestamp we control, plus a per-adapter start date, makes lifespan analysis exact and makes the window trap impossible to fall into twice.
  5. Detect re-listings, don't count them as closures. At least 29.2% of eFinancialCareers closures are the same role under a new ID. Users currently see those as expired. Matching on employer plus normalised title at write time would keep the role alive in the product and stop the churn metrics from overstating turnover.
  6. Normalise skill casing at write time. 2,549 labels currently fragment across capitalisation variants, which understates every skill frequency in the product's own filters.
  7. Precision-audit the fuzzy cross-post matcher. It is responsible for ~2,150 of 2,304 merge decisions and has no measured precision. Label 200 pairs by hand and find out whether it is hiding real roles.
  8. Capture applicant counts longitudinally. The urgency question stays unanswerable while we only see applicant counts on still-open roles. Snapshotting the count per job per day would resolve it in a month and turn the null result above into a real finding either way.
